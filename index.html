    Scientific rationale

    Dust is a fundamental component of galaxies: it absorbs ultraviolet radiation and re-emits it in the infrared, regulates the cooling of the interstellar medium and star formation, and traces the cycle of metals. Yet how dust forms and evolves in the first billion years of the Universe, and what role it plays across galaxy evolution, remain among the outstanding challenges in astrophysics.

    The advent of JWST and ALMA has transformed the field. ALMA has revealed substantial dust reservoirs already in place at z > 6, implying rapid enrichment and efficient grain growth, and has found dust in environments once thought hostile to grain survival: quasar hosts, the circumgalactic medium and quiescent galaxies. JWST now places direct constraints on extinction curves, metallicities and gas conditions in early galaxies, and probes dust composition and the presence of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) at high redshift.

    The emerging picture is complex and sometimes contradictory. Some galaxies at z ~ 7 already host large dust masses, while others show low dust-to-metal ratios; UV-luminous galaxies at z > 10 often appear surprisingly dust-poor. AGN add a further layer of uncertainty: the relative contributions of star formation and AGN heating to the infrared emission of luminous quasars are still poorly constrained, and so is the impact of AGN on the removal of dust and PAHs in massive galaxies.

    On the theory side, galaxy-evolution simulations are moving beyond simple scaling relations towards explicit modelling of the dust lifecycle, including grain growth and destruction, the evolution of grain sizes and chemistry, PAHs, and realistic AGN feedback. Dust studies have turned from a detection problem into a problem of physical interpretation: dust is there early on, but its formation pathways, its composition and its impact on galaxy evolution remain unclear.

    This focus week brings together a small group of observers and theorists (~25 participants) working with JWST, ALMA and state-of-the-art simulations, to discuss recent advances, identify the most pressing open questions and define strategies for future observational and theoretical efforts.

    Working themes

    01

    ALMA and JWST for dust and PAHs

    What have we learned about dust from JWST and ALMA?

    The synergy between ALMA far-infrared data and JWST spectroscopy now constrains dust masses, attenuation laws and grain properties at z > 6.

    • How rapidly can dust build up within the first billion years?
    • What do extinction curves and UV bumps reveal about grain composition and size distribution?
    • Are PAHs and other carbonaceous grains already common at high redshift?
    • How do dust properties vary from UV-bright to heavily obscured galaxies?

    02

    Dust & AGN

    What is the role of AGN in shaping dust properties?

    Radiation from accreting black holes can heat dust and alter its observable properties, while AGN-driven winds may destroy or redistribute it.

    • How much of the infrared emission of high-z quasars comes from star formation, and how much from AGN heating?
    • Do AGN-driven outflows destroy dust, or redistribute it within the host galaxy?
    • Can dust properties tell starburst- and AGN-dominated systems apart?

    03

    Are we ready for high-z?

    Are our simulations ready for the high-redshift Universe?

    The physics in current models is still simplified and uncertain, and simulations are not always compared with observations on equal terms.

    • What is the best way to compare simulation predictions with the new data, and why?
    • Can simulations reproduce dust abundance, emission and attenuation at high z at the same time?
    • Which modelling strategies should the next generation of simulations prioritise?
    • Which ALMA and JWST observations can test or falsify current models?

    Format & goals

    The week is built around extensive, active discussion between observers and theorists. Mornings open with introductory seminars (~40 min) that summarise the state of the art on each theme, followed by talks (15–20 min) presenting new results. Afternoons are devoted to long interactive discussions, brainstorming, and working sessions where small mixed groups of observers and simulators work together.

    Dedicated time will go to coordinated proposal planning for the upcoming ALMA, ESO and JWST calls: the week falls about two months before the 2027 ALMA deadline. The goal is to shape new projects, proposals and papers, and a summary of the discussions and outcomes will be shared with the community.

    • Seminars & talks
    • Interactive discussions
    • Brainstorming sessions
    • Small-group work
    • Proposal planning
